EPISODE 1 — THE LEGEND OF THE MONKEYS FROM GIBRALTAR

INTRODUCTION On Gibraltar there exists a story that has never been fully confirmed, yet has never been completely disproven either. Some say it began as a joke — a brief answer given somewhere across the border in Spain, when the question was asked: “When will the British leave Gibraltar?” The answer was: “When the monkeys leave.” Over time, that sentence began to take on a life of its own. It was no longer just a joke. It started appearing in reports, in soldiers’ conversations, and in administrative notes within the garrison. During the Second World War, Gibraltar became one of the most heavily monitored strategic points in Europe. But in parallel, something occurred that official accounts never fully explained: subtle changes in the behavior and numbers of the monkeys inhabiting the Rock. Their population declined. And with it — tension began to rise. In some historical interpretations, efforts were made to preserve or restore the presence of Barbary macaques on the Rock. Whether this was policy, precaution, or tradition remains debated.
CHAPTER 1 — THE BEGINNING OF THE CONFLICT At the beginning of the 18th century, Great Britain seized Gibraltar — a key strategic point at the Strait of Gibraltar. This narrow passage connected the Atlantic Ocean and the Mediterranean Sea, making Gibraltar one of the most important naval positions in Europe. Over time, Gibraltar became a Royal Navy stronghold and a symbol of maritime power. Extensive fortifications and tunnels transformed it into a military fortress.
CHAPTER 2 — THE MONKEYS OF THE ROCK Gibraltar is home to Barbary macaques — the only wild monkeys in Europe. Soldiers observed them daily. Visitors documented them. Over time, a belief emerged: “As long as the monkeys remain, Gibraltar remains stable.” By the early 20th century, they were no longer just animals — they became part of Gibraltar’s identity.
CHAPTER 3 — EARLY INTERPRETIVE FORMATION During the war period, changes in macaque population began to be recorded. These changes were interpreted as meaningful, even without confirmed causal links. Population shifts became symbolic signals within military observation systems.
CHAPTER 4 — OPERATIONAL CROSSING Gibraltar became a system where environmental observation and interpretation overlapped. Macaque population changes were tracked and analyzed repeatedly. No single cause was ever confirmed. But interpretation created meaning where none was officially assigned.
CHAPTER 5 — THE EMERGENCE OF THE NARRATIVE EFFECT By World War II, the monkeys were no longer just wildlife. They became part of operational awareness. Protection measures increased. Monitoring intensified. Interpretation shaped response.
CHAPTER 6 — STABILIZATION THROUGH INTERPRETATION By 1942, Gibraltar operated under dual structure: military infrastructure and interpretive perception. The presence of monkeys became a constant variable in cultural and administrative awareness. Over time, observation itself became part of the system.
EPILOGUE — THE EXTINCTION OF THE QUESTION Gibraltar is no longer defined by a question about monkeys. The question still exists, but it is no longer operational. It remains as history, memory, and interpretation layered together.
